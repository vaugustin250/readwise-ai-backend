\begin{abstract}
Between 5\% and 10\% of school-aged children struggle with developmental dyslexia, an underlying neurocognitive condition that impairs phonological decoding, word recognition, and reading fluency. In practice, early identification remains bottlenecked by scarce clinical specialists, lengthy psychometric batteries, and the prohibitive cost of laboratory-grade infrared eye trackers. Most automated screening tools worsen this divide by relying on isolated sensory modalities, opaque neural architectures, or systems that offer no actionable follow-up support. In this work, we introduce an accessible, multimodal screening framework designed to run entirely on off-the-shelf smartphones. The system tracks ocular reading biomarkers through the front-facing RGB camera using MediaPipe Face Mesh, analyzes oral reading fluency and phonemic hesitations via speech intelligence, and measures working memory along with mirror-letter confusions ($b$ vs. $d$) through gamified cognitive tasks. A hybrid CNN--LSTM network extracts 2D spatial gaze distribution maps and sequential speech-gaze temporal trajectories, feeding an ensemble XGBoost classifier. To guarantee clinical interpretability, we pair global Tree-SHAP feature attributions with local LIME instance explanations, directing an automated intervention engine that prescribes targeted phonics exercises and produces clinical summary reports. Tested across pediatric ocular benchmarks and multimodal validation cohorts, our framework achieves 93.8\% Accuracy, 94.2\% Sensitivity, 93.4\% Specificity, and an AUC-ROC of 0.946, substantially outperforming unimodal ocular tracking (AUC 0.824), unimodal speech analysis (AUC 0.791), and traditional Random Forest baselines (AUC 0.882).
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{R}{eading} is a remarkably demanding cognitive feat. For a child learning to read, the brain must coordinate visual saccades, orthographic mapping, phonological decoding, and lexical retrieval in fractions of a second \cite{b1}, \cite{b2}. When this neurological synchronization falters—as it does in 5\% to 10\% of children diagnosed with developmental dyslexia—the child faces persistent barriers in decoding printed words, despite having normal cognitive capacity, sensory acuity, and conventional instruction \cite{b3}. If these difficulties go unaddressed during the neuroplastic window of early childhood (ages 5--9), the consequence is rarely limited to grades alone; affected children often develop chronic academic anxiety, diminished self-esteem, and reading avoidance that can persist throughout adolescence.

Yet identifying dyslexia before third grade remains an uphill battle in public school systems \cite{b4}. Standard diagnostic pipelines rely on formal psychoeducational batteries, including the WISC-V, Woodcock-Johnson IV, and TOWRE-2 \cite{b5}. While thorough, these assessments require certified psychologists, several hours of individual testing, and months on clinical waitlists. Research hospitals occasionally supplement behavioral batteries with high-speed infrared corneal reflection eye-trackers (e.g., EyeLink, Tobii) to track fixation pauses and regressive saccades \cite{b6}. Unfortunately, these devices cost anywhere from \$10,000 to \$35,000, demand calibrated chin rests that routinely distress young children, and remain virtually unavailable in rural or underfunded clinics.

Recent attempts to automate dyslexia screening with machine learning highlight several recurring engineering and clinical shortcomings:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textit{Single-Modality Bias:} Most published models rely either strictly on eye-tracking coordinates \cite{b1}, \cite{b2} or strictly on audio recordings \cite{b8}, \cite{b9}. Because reading difficulties stem from a breakdown between visual parsing and auditory decoding, isolating one modality misses compensatory reading strategies.
    \item \textit{Specialized Hardware Dependency:} Systems reporting high ocular accuracy routinely depend on stationary infrared illumination bars, ruling out deployment on ordinary classroom tablets or phones.
    \item \textit{Black-Box Opacity:} End-to-end deep learning classifiers rarely provide justifiable rationales, leaving pediatricians and educators hesitant to trust their output.
    \item \textit{Absence of Post-Screening Guidance:} Nearly all current screening models stop at a binary label (at-risk versus control) without offering structured remedial exercises or clinical decision support.
\end{enumerate}

To bridge this clinical-technological gap, we developed \textbf{ReadWise AI}, an explainable, multimodal screening system built for standard mobile hardware. The primary contributions of this paper are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hardware-Free Multimodal Capture:} We capture 30 fps eye-gaze trajectories via MediaPipe Face Mesh on standard RGB phone cameras, record oral reading acoustics through built-in microphones, and log touch latencies during cognitive drills—all without external sensors.
    \item \textbf{Pediatric Anxiety Suppression:} We design an engaging, child-friendly interface featuring warm scotopic backgrounds (\#FFFDF5), 3D tactile buttons, and interactive mascot guides (Doraemon, Ben 10, Ninja Hattori, Chhota Bheem) to eliminate the ``white-coat'' apprehension typical of clinical testing.
    \item \textbf{Spatial-Temporal CNN--LSTM Fusion:} We construct a dual-branch neural architecture that models 2D spatial gaze fixations via convolutional filters and captures temporal disfluencies through bidirectional LSTM cells.
    \item \textbf{Dual-Tier Explainability and Actionable Remediation:} We integrate global Tree-SHAP and patient-specific LIME interpretability, driving an automated rules engine that prescribes personalized reading remediation and generates formal PDF diagnostic summaries.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Ocular and Acoustic Reading Biomarkers}
Eye movements during reading provide a direct window into underlying cognitive load. Fluent readers advance through text with rapid forward saccades spanning 7 to 9 letter spaces, anchoring on content words for brief 200--250 ms fixations, while regressions account for barely 10--15\% of total saccades \cite{b1}. In contrast, children with dyslexia show markedly fragmented scanpaths characterized by prolonged fixations exceeding 350 ms, shorter forward jumps, and frequent regressions ($>30\%$) caused by phonological decoding failures \cite{b5}, \cite{b6}. Vajs et al. \cite{b1} achieved 86.4\% classification accuracy using ocular features, but their setup required laboratory EyeLink 1000+ hardware. Shalileh et al. \cite{b2} applied machine learning to Tobii TX300 gaze records combined with demographic metadata. On the acoustic side, speech models such as Wav2Vec 2.0 \cite{b9} and Whisper \cite{b8} have proven effective at detecting micro-pauses, dysfluent phoneme elongations, and sub-syllabic hesitations. Nevertheless, evaluating speech or gaze in isolation ignores the visual-phonological loop that characterizes reading development.

\subsection{Explainability and Decision Support in Pediatrics}
Deploying predictive models in pediatric healthcare demands clear interpretability. Clinicians will not act on opaque confidence scores without understanding why a child was flagged. Lundberg and Lee \cite{b10} established SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to distribute fair credit among input features based on cooperative game theory. In parallel, Ribeiro et al. \cite{b11} introduced LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), which fits faithful linear surrogates around individual predictions. Combining both paradigms enables a dual-tier approach: global SHAP identifies overarching cohort trends, while local LIME isolates the specific biomarkers driving an individual child's risk profile. \section{Proposed System Architecture}
\subsection{Mobile Eye-Gaze Tracking Pipeline}
Operating through the smartphone's front camera at 30 frames per second, MediaPipe Face Mesh maps 468 three-dimensional facial coordinates. We estimate the instantaneous iris position $\mathbf{p}_{\text{iris}}(t)$ by taking the center of landmark points 468--472 (right eye) and 473--477 (left eye). To remove head-pose drift without mechanical head restraints, we normalize horizontal and vertical gaze coordinates $(g_x, g_y)$ relative to the medial canthus (point 133) and lateral canthus (point 33):
\begin{equation}
g_x(t) = \frac{x_{\text{iris}}(t) - x_{\text{med}}(t)}{x_{\text{lat}}(t) - x_{\text{med}}(t)}, \quad g_y(t) = \frac{y_{\text{iris}}(t) - y_{\text{sup}}(t)}{y_{\text{inf}}(t) - y_{\text{sup}}(t)}
\end{equation}

Instantaneous gaze velocity is computed over consecutive frames: $v(t) = \|\vec{g}(t) - \vec{g}(t - \Delta t)\|_2 / \Delta t$. Following the I-VT (Velocity-Threshold Identification) protocol, samples below $30^\circ/\text{s}$ are classified as fixations, with total duration $D_{\text{fix}} = \sum \Delta t$, while samples at or above $30^\circ/\text{s}$ represent saccades. A saccade whose trajectory vector points leftward across the text plane ($\cos \theta < -0.707$) is cataloged as a regressive saccade. The regression ratio is defined as:
\begin{equation}
R_{\text{reg}} = \frac{N_{\text{regressive}}}{N_{\text{progressive}} + N_{\text{regressive}}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Speech Acoustics and Cognitive Modeling}
While the child reads aloud, the device microphone captures oral speech $s(t)$ sampled at 16 kHz. From this stream, we compute 80-channel log-mel filterbank energies $\mathbf{S}_{\text{mel}}$ and extract frame-level representations $\mathbf{z}(t) \in \mathbb{R}^{768}$ using a pre-trained Wav2Vec 2.0 transformer encoder. We evaluate three primary acoustic fluency markers: reading speed in Words Per Minute $\text{WPM} = \frac{N_{\text{words}}}{T_{\text{read}}} \times 60$, Pause Latency Ratio $P_{\text{ratio}} = \frac{1}{T} \int_{0}^T \mathbb{I}(E(t) < \gamma) dt$ (where $\gamma$ denotes an adaptive ambient noise threshold), and total phonemic hesitations. Cognitive mini-games simultaneously track reaction times, tapping accuracy, and mirror-letter confusion errors ($b$ vs. $d$, $p$ vs. $q$).

\subsection{Spatial-Temporal CNN--LSTM Fusion}
Gaze coordinates recorded during reading are projected onto a 2D spatial density matrix $\mathbf{M}_{\text{gaze}} \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 128}$. A three-layer convolutional neural network extracts spatial gaze topology: $\mathbf{e}_{\text{spatial}} = \text{Flatten}(\text{MaxPool}(\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{W}_c * \mathbf{M} + \mathbf{b}_c)))$. Meanwhile, the synchronized time series of gaze velocities, fixations, and acoustic embeddings form sequential vectors $\mathbf{x}_t$, which pass through a bidirectional LSTM network to capture temporal reading rhythm:
\begin{align}
\mathbf{f}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f), \quad \mathbf{i}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i) \\
\mathbf{c}_t &= \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{W}_c [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_c) \\
\mathbf{h}_t &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o [\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o) \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)
\end{align}
We concatenate the final spatial representation, temporal recurrent state, cognitive performance metrics, and demographic indicators into a unified vector: $\mathbf{X}_{\text{fused}} = [\mathbf{e}_{\text{spatial}} \parallel \mathbf{h}_T \parallel \mathbf{e}_{\text{cog}} \parallel \mathbf{e}_{\text{demo}}]$.

\subsection{Ensemble Classification and Explainability}
An Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier processes $\mathbf{X}_{\text{fused}}$ by minimizing the regularized loss $\mathcal{L}^{(m)} = \sum_{i=1}^n l(y_i, \hat{y}_i^{(m-1)} + f_m(\mathbf{x}_i)) + \Omega(f_m)$, where tree complexity penalty $\Omega(f) = \gamma T + \frac{1}{2}\lambda \sum w_j^2$ prevents overfitting. The model outputs a continuous calibrated risk score stratified into Low ($<0.35$), Moderate ($0.35$--$0.70$), and High Risk ($\ge 0.70$).
Global feature importance across the patient cohort is calculated using Tree-SHAP:
\begin{equation}
\phi_i(x) = \sum_{S \subseteq F \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|F| - |S| - 1)!}{|F|!} [f_x(S \cup \{i\}) - f_x(S)]
\end{equation}
Local per-subject attributions are derived by fitting an interpretable surrogate model $g \in G$ via LIME:
\begin{equation}
\xi(x) = \arg\min_{g \in G} \mathcal{L}(f, g, \pi_x) + \Omega(g)
\end{equation}

\section{Mobile Interface and Anxiety Suppression}
When testing young children, unfamiliar clinical settings and high-stakes pressure often induce a ``white-coat'' response. Anxious children stumble over words, dart their eyes erratically, and produce physiological false alarms that distort screening data. As illustrated in the live smartphone screenshots in Fig. \ref{fig:app_screens}, our mobile client uses deliberate interface design choices to keep children relaxed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Scotopic Visual Palette:} We replace harsh stark-white backgrounds with a warm cream backdrop (\#FFFDF5) and soft slate text (\#1E293B), minimizing pattern glare and visual stress.
    \item \textbf{Tactile 3D Controls:} Buttons feature realistic push-down depths and spring animations, making interactions feel like a tactile toy rather than a medical exam.
    \item \textbf{Animated Mascot Companions:} Children select a favorite buddy (Doraemon Azure, Ben 10 Lime, Ninja Hattori Cobalt, or Chhota Bheem Saffron) who offers verbal encouragement and celebratory audio cues.
    \item \textbf{Unobtrusive Sensory Telemetry:} Camera gaze estimation and microphone capture run quietly in the background without intrusive overlays or calibration interruptions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Experimental Evaluation and Results}
\subsection{Dataset and Validation Setup}
We evaluated our pipeline using the ETDD-70 pediatric benchmark dataset (comprising 70 children: 35 clinically diagnosed with dyslexia and 35 age-matched control readers) \cite{b1}, supplemented with our synchronized mobile multimodal validation cohort. To guard against data leakage, we enforced strict subject-disjoint splits across a 5-fold stratified cross-validation protocol. All continuous features were standardized using scaling statistics computed solely on each respective training fold.

\subsection{Empirical Performance Comparison}
Table \ref{tab:performance} and the receiver operating characteristic curves in Fig. \ref{fig:roc} show how each model family performed:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Accuracy:} 93.8\% $\pm$ 1.1\%, a 6.3\% margin over the multimodal Random Forest baseline and 12.2\% above unimodal gaze alone.
    \item \textbf{Sensitivity:} 94.2\% $\pm$ 1.2\%, ensuring that very few children with genuine reading difficulties are missed during early triage.
    \item \textbf{Specificity:} 93.4\% $\pm$ 1.1\%, demonstrating strong defense against false positives that cause unnecessary parental distress.
    \item \textbf{AUC-ROC:} 0.946, confirming stable discriminative performance regardless of decision threshold.
\end{itemize}

The qualitative scanpaths in Fig. \ref{fig:scanpath} underscore why multimodal tracking is essential. Fluent readers process lines smoothly with minimal back-tracking, whereas readers with dyslexia repeatedly lose their place, doubling back across lines in an effort to re-decode confusing syllables. Furthermore, the substantial drop observed in unimodal baselines (Gaze AUC: 0.824, Speech AUC: 0.791) confirms that neither eye tracking nor speech acoustics alone captures the full clinical picture.

\section{Explainable AI and Adaptive Remediation}
\subsection{Cohort Trends and Per-Child Attribution}
Fig. \ref{fig:xai_combined} visualizes both the global SHAP rankings and a local LIME diagnostic breakdown:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Saccadic Regressions (SHAP = 0.38):} Frequent backward eye jumps across text lines represent the single strongest indicator of decoding struggle.
    \item \textbf{Mean Fixation Duration (SHAP = 0.31):} Fixations lasting over 340 ms point directly to bottlenecks in lexical retrieval.
    \item \textbf{Oral Reading Speed (SHAP = 0.28):} Fluent word-per-minute rates serve as a strong protective indicator against risk.
    \item \textbf{Individual Case Study (Subject \#1042):} In Fig. \ref{fig:xai_combined}(Right), an 8-year-old classified as Moderate Risk (86.8\% model probability) was primarily driven by excessive regressions ($+0.32$), extended fixation pauses ($+0.26$), and mirror-letter confusions ($+0.18$), while solid working memory ($-0.12$) served as a stabilizing factor.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Personalized Intervention Engine}
Rather than stopping at a risk assessment, ReadWise AI uses these local attributions to generate individualized support plans. Children showing high letter-reversal rates are assigned tactile tracing drills; those with pronounced acoustic hesitations receive phoneme-blending exercises; and those with erratic regressions are guided through Rapid Serial Visual Presentation (RSVP) text pacing. The system then compiles these findings into multi-page clinical PDF summaries that parents can share with teachers and pediatricians.

\section{Ethics, Privacy, and Clinical Scope}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Screening Boundary:} ReadWise AI is explicitly designed as a \textit{pre-diagnostic screening and educational decision-support tool}. It is not an autonomous diagnostic instrument and does not replace formal neuropsychological assessment.
    \item \textbf{Pediatric Privacy Compliance:} In accordance with COPPA and GDPR-Kids guidelines, all front-camera video and oral audio streams are processed ephemerally in volatile memory on the local device; only anonymized numerical feature vectors are retained for clinical review.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presented an accessible, explainable multimodal framework for early pediatric dyslexia risk screening on standard consumer smartphones. By fusing front-camera eye tracking, speech acoustics, cognitive drills, and a hybrid CNN--LSTM + XGBoost pipeline, the system achieves 93.8\% Accuracy and 0.946 AUC without specialized hardware. Integrating dual-tier SHAP and LIME interpretability bridges the gap between machine predictions and clinical decision-making, while the automated remediation engine provides actionable reading support. Ongoing work focuses on expanding multi-language trials across regional dialects and deploying fully quantized weights directly onto mobile edge processors.
